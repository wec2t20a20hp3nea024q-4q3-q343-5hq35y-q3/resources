Cycle 1: Using toehold switches to detect CKD

Design: Justifying the use of toehold switches + Choosing miRNAs

Inspiration for the Project

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is a prevalent disease in Hong Kong affecting around 10% of our population. The scale of this problem is ever growing. CKD cases in Hong Kong soared by over 180,000 from 2021 to 2025. This sharp increase leads to three major issues: a heavy strain on dialysis units, significant financial burdens for patients, and a high death rate. In 2024, kidney-related conditions were the 6th leading cause of death in Hong Kong, with 1,700 deaths, and 70% of it is related to CKD. Early detection is crucial for saving lives and alleviating pressure on the healthcare system.

What is miRNA?

miRNA (microRNA) is a type of ribonucleic acid found in plants and animals. It acts like a control switch which binds to messenger RNA to turn genes off, blocking protein production. miRNA levels may be upregulated or downregulated when a patient is infected with diseases, and may also contribute to conditions such as cancer and heart problems.

Why did we choose miRNA as our detection target?

miRNAs are often upregulated or downregulated in diseased states and act as a reporter for specific diseases, including but not limited to:

  • Cancers
  • Cardiovascular disease
  • Neurological diseases
  • Infectious diseases

Pros and Cons of miRNA as a Disease Reporter

Pros Cons
High stability Enclosed in microvesicles or bound to proteins, miRNAs resist degradation by enzymes (RNases) in blood and urine. Complex signals One miRNA targets many genes, and one gene is controlled by many miRNAs, clouding disease-specific signs.
Early warning Their levels often shift before standard clinical indicators, such as serum creatinine or significant albuminuria, show measurable changes. Outside interference Inflammation, other illnesses, and common drugs alter miRNA levels, causing false readings.
Disease specificity Specific patterns of dysregulated miRNAs correspond directly to underlying pathological processes like renal fibrosis, inflammation, and diabetic nephropathy.
Non-invasive access Urinary and circulating miRNAs can be sampled easily and repeatedly without requiring painful or risky kidney biopsies.

Why did we consider a multi-miRNA system?

We acknowledged that using only one miRNA will not be sufficient to detect CKD. A multi-miRNA system uses several miRNAs together, improving disease detection, boosting gene knockdown, and targeting complex biological pathways, which is much better than using a single-miRNA panel, as miRNAs are not specific enough to detect only CKD. The accuracy of a single-miRNA panel would be far lower than a multi-miRNA system.

Since CKD is not a single disease, but the final common pathway of several underlying conditions, no miRNA is totally specific to CKD. Therefore, we adopted a multi-miRNA system to check for its most common pathways for CKD, namely:

  • Hypertensive nephropathy
  • Diabetic nephropathy

In this situation, using a multi-miRNA system increases reliability and accuracy for the detection of CKD.

Which miRNAs did we choose?

In our original screening we found 5 miRNAs related to CKD: miR-21-5p, miR-192-5p, miR-204-5p, miR-214-3p and miR-193a-3p. However, our target miRNA needed to meet two criteria:

  • Shows significant upregulation of concentration compared to healthy controls in the urine samples of CKD patients
  • Needs to be caught in early to mid stages of CKD

During the second stage of our literature review, we discovered that miR-204-5p and miR-214-3p appeared to be downregulated instead of being upregulated, making it difficult to detect via our current toehold design. Among the remaining 3 miRNAs, miR-193a-3p is found to be only upregulated by 1.4-fold. Compared to miR-21-5p's 2–5× increase and miR-192-5p's 3× increase, our final choice landed on miR-21-5p and miR-192-5p.

Limitations of current medical detection methods (eGFR and uACR)

  • Delayed detection: eGFR only measures the filtration rate rather than structural kidney damage. Significant kidney injury can occur long before the eGFR drops below normal levels.
  • Misses early damage: Standard cutoffs classify uACR levels below 30 mg/g as completely normal. However, early kidney damage and cardiovascular risk can progress below this threshold.

What is a Toehold Switch?

A toehold switch is a type of detection method for the upregulation of miRNA. Our team has used a toehold switch mechanism to create a test kit for CKD.

Mechanism of Toehold Switches

Toehold switches are de-novo-designed synthetic riboregulators (custom-made RNA switches, from sketch, that turn bacterial genes on/off) that activate gene expression in response to cognate RNAs with designed sequences. The toehold switch has a ribosome-binding site (RBS) and a start codon hidden inside the hairpin. When there is no trigger miRNA present, the ribosome cannot bind to the RBS, and hence no reporter protein will be produced. When the specific trigger miRNA binds to the toehold at the 5′ end, it opens the hairpin by branch migration; therefore, ribosomes can bind to the RBS, starting translation and producing reporter proteins.

Rationale for Selecting Toehold Switches

  1. High specificity: detects exact 22-nucleotide miRNA sequences.
  2. Non-invasive sampling: works with urine; a blood draw is not needed.
  3. Programmability: enabling precise gene regulation and simultaneous detection of multiple miRNA targets.
  4. Minimal crosstalk: each trigger usually interacts only with its matching switch.
  5. Low-cost format: can be deployed on paper test strips.

Limitation: weak signals at early disease stages; limited sensitivity for low-abundance miRNAs.

Build: Constructing toehold switches by using miRADAR

miRADAR (from the 2024 Wageningen iGEM project) explores miRNAs as potential biomarkers. The system uses RNA-responsive toehold switches to detect miRNAs and convert their presence into a measurable output. We computationally designed toehold switches targeting associated miRNAs and experimentally evaluated their performance. These experiments assessed whether computationally designed, miRNA-responsive toehold switches could function as RNA sensors.

Test: Using NUPACK to analyse our toehold switches

For the dry lab component, we used miRADAR and NUPACK to generate and evaluate sample toehold switches for miR-21-5p and miR-192-5p. Samples were validated in NUPACK at 37 °C (RNA mode), with toehold concentration of 1 μM and miRNA concentration of 3 μM.

Criteria for choosing suitable toehold switches:

  1. A high concentration of bound toeholds is formed. This ensures that the miRNA can bind to the toehold switch and unwind the hairpin stem to release the ribosome-binding site for the ribosome to bind and produce eGFP.
  2. No / little unintended secondary structures are formed in the bound toeholds. Extra base-pairing after the RBS could block the ribosome from reaching the coding sequence and cause false negatives.
  3. HMFE vs TMFE: if TMFE is lower than HMFE, the toeholds would prioritise binding with another toehold switch instead of forming hybrids with the miRNA.

Figure 13 (right): Complex formation under NUPACK

As seen from the above pictures, both toeholds, especially toehold 9, bind with miR-21 exceptionally well. The efficient binding of both toehold and miRNA ensures that our kit won't miss out on much chemical signals from our consumers and little miRNAs are left unbound.

Another property that we prioritised in our design is a stable bond when the toehold is unbound. Making our toehold unlikely to self-linearize on its own without any miRNA signals and indicate a false positive.

Bonds holding the loop structure in the toehold also need a low equilibrium probability. This has 2 advantages. For one, it leaves the loop able to open more readily, exposing the Ribosome Binding Site (RBS) to commence protein synthesis for our colour change. We can observe this property in the pictures below as the light blue colours at the beginning of each loop; light blue indicates a low equilibrium probability in NUPACK. In addition to the loop being opened more readily, low equilibrium probability prevents the issue in which the bondings within the loop are too strong for the toehold to linearise properly.

Figure 13b (left): Hybrids of Toehold 7 for miR-21

Figure 13b (right): Hybrids of Toehold 9 for miR-21

Our toeholds needed to have a lower minimum free energy (MFE) while bonded as opposed to being unbound since this complies with thermodynamics and makes the bound state more favourable to form compared to the unbound state.

Compared with the other toehold switches, Toeholds 7 and 9 demonstrate more efficient miRNA binding, leaving minimal unbound miRNA and resulting in a high concentration of the active toehold-miRNA complex. This efficiency and precise activation are driven by the following structural and thermodynamic mechanisms:

  • Thermodynamic Stability: The bound toehold-miRNA complex possesses a lower minimum free energy (MFE) than the unbound toehold switch, energetically favoring the bound state.
  • RBS Sequestration: In the unbound state, the ribosome binding site (RBS) is sequestered within a loop structure rather than an exposed linear conformation. This minimizes unintended ribosome binding and background expression in the absence of the target miRNA.
  • Structural Linearization: Once the miRNA target binds, the base pairs maintaining the secondary loop structures shift to a lower equilibrium probability. This destabilizes the loops, enabling the toehold switch to linearize and fully expose the RBS so the ribosome can initiate reporter protein synthesis.

Cycle 2: Experimenting toehold switches

Design: Cloning rationale for eGFP

Plasmid selection

We selected plasmid pET-15b-EGFP. It contains a T7 promoter, which utilizes T7 RNA polymerase to drive strong and inducible reactions and is superior to typical host bacterial enzymes. An eGFP reporter gene produces a sensitive and quantifiable output, while a T7 terminator stops RNA production. The plasmid also contains an AmpR gene which provides the bacteria with ampicillin resistance so that they may still survive on agar plates containing ampicillin.

Cloning of plasmid (Digestion)

We utilized restriction enzyme sites XbaI and NdeI. XbaI recognizes and cleaves 5′-TCTAGA-3′; NdeI recognizes and cleaves 5′-CATATG-3′. Both sites are downstream of the T7 promoter and upstream of the eGFP reporter, allowing the T7 promoter to synthesize our toehold switch, the eGFP reporter to be expressed, and the T7 terminator to stop synthesis.

Gel electrophoresis, purification and ligation

After digestion, gel electrophoresis was used to separate the digestion byproducts from the digested plasmid. We then purified the gel to remove any unnecessary waste. Once the gel was purified, we attempted ligation to insert the toehold switch into the plasmid and conducted a transformation to insert the plasmids into E. coli for duplication of plasmid samples.

Transformation and plating results

After ligation, we conducted transformation to duplicate our plasmid samples. First, we heat-shocked competent cells to temporarily induce cell membrane permeability. Then, after mixing the ligation samples, we plated the resultant mixture onto the agar plates. For miR-192-5p, colonies were found on plates containing toehold 6 and toehold 7.

Figure 16: Transformation plates (positive / negative controls and transformed colonies)

Picking colonies and miniprep

After picking colonies, we conducted miniprep to purify and extract the DNA plasmids from the colonies for plate reading. However, the results were suboptimal as the concentration of the DNA plasmids was low. We suspected the result may be due to:

  • A lack of plasmid samples due to the use of small collection tubes.
  • The failure of the ligation process, i.e. the toehold switch failed to be inserted between restriction sites XbaI and NdeI.
  • The lack of incubation time of culture, resulting in suboptimal concentration.

The experiment was then repeated to obtain better results. To improve the accuracy of our experiment, we conducted some modifications:

  • Using larger tubes to produce more plasmids.
  • Longer incubation time of culture, allowing the bacteria to divide and produce more plasmids.
  • Increasing the concentration of restriction enzymes XbaI and NdeI and ligase so the plasmid can be digested and the toehold switch inserted.

Retransformation of DNA plasmids

We then retransformed some of the plasmid DNA from miniprep to duplicate our samples. After retransformation, colonies were only found on toehold 6 samples. We then picked 2 colonies and conducted miniprep with the overnight culture. The results of miniprep were promising as we obtained large concentrations and optimum purity (A260/280).

After ligating the pET-15b-EGFP plasmid with Toehold Switch 6 and Toehold Switch 7, we transformed E. coli with the recombinant plasmids: Toehold Switch 6 plasmids (T6 plasmids) and Toehold Switch 7 plasmids (T7 plasmids). The bacteria were then cultured on agar plates containing LB medium and ampicillin. We also set up two controls: a positive control with bacteria transformed with the pET-15b-EGFP plasmid (non-recombinant) and a negative control with only bacteria.

Test: eGFP cell free expression assay

The EGFP cell-free assay makes use of a fluorometer to detect the specific fluorescence inside the samples. To initiate the reaction, the fluorometer exposes the sample to a specific wavelength so the electrons inside the sample absorb the energy, which then emits waves with longer wavelengths and less energy. These waves can then be detected by the fluorometer through a GFP filter.

We had positive and negative sets for each toehold switch and control setup. The corresponding miRNA was added to the positive sets and was absent in the negative sets. Plasmids pET-15b-EGFP with or without toehold switches were used. We then used a microplate reader and fluorometer to measure the light intensity of the fluorescence. However, minimal fold change was observed.

Although we repeated the experiment several times to increase reliability, results show a trend of increase that did not achieve statistical significance in most samples. We suspect the results are due to:

  • Pipetting errors, such as including bubbles inside the sample, which decreases fluorescence intensity.
  • A lack of trials, which lowers the reliability of the statistics.
  • The design of the toehold switches may not be optimal.

Learn

  • Acknowledged that using EGFP would only give a minimal change in production in the presence of miRNA.
  • Suggested using mCherry as the reporter protein to produce colorimetric results.

Cycle 3: Using mCherry to produce colorimetric result

Design: Cloning rationale of mCherry plasmid

To improve the user experience and practical accessibility of our diagnostic kit, we hypothesised that altering our reporter protein would yield a more intuitive and user-friendly readout. The primary objective of this cycle was to redesign our plasmid architecture to incorporate mCherry as the designated reporter. The rationale driving this modification is that mCherry, upon adequate expression and cellular accumulation, generates a distinct colorimetric output that is visible under standard ambient lighting. By cloning the mCherry sequence into our system, we aimed to engineer a biological sensor capable of providing clear, naked-eye confirmation of miRNA detection, completely bypassing the need for specialised laboratory hardware.

Learn: From toehold switch to HCR

Lack of visible result from toehold switch

Through evaluation of our original toehold-switch design, we identified a practical limitation associated with using eGFP as the reporter. Although successful activation of the toehold switch can lead to eGFP production, the resulting fluorescence is not readily visible under normal lighting conditions and generally requires appropriate excitation with a specific wavelength and fluorescence-detection equipment to observe. This means that successful molecular detection does not necessarily translate into a result that can be easily interpreted by the user. From this, we learned that the current method is effective at target recognition, but lacks a directly visible method for signal visualization.

Insufficient sensitivity at low miRNA concentration

mCherry was considered as an alternative to eGFP since sufficiently accumulated mCherry can produce a visible colour under normal lighting conditions. However, achieving a clearly visible colour requires substantial reporter accumulation. This exposes another hurdle: at low target miRNA concentrations, reporter production may be insufficient to generate a perceivable output. This is particularly relevant to our aim of detecting disease at the early-to-mid stage, where the abundance of the selected miRNA biomarkers may be relatively low. Therefore, we identified signal amplification as an additional design requirement to improve detection sensitivity.

Two design challenges identified:

  1. A readout that can be easily observed.
  2. Sufficient signal amplification at low target miRNA concentration.

Cycle 4: From toehold switches to using HCR to detect miRNA

Design: Justifying the use of HCR

After evaluating the limitations of reporter-based toehold switches, we explored Hybridization Chain Reaction (HCR) as an alternative miRNA detection method.

Mechanism of HCR

HCR is an enzyme-free signal amplification technique that relies on target-triggered hybridisation between designed DNA hairpins, resulting in the formation of long nicked double-stranded DNA polymers. By using two complementary hairpins, the opening of one hairpin exposes a sequence that can trigger the opening of the next, allowing the reaction to continue in a chain. Through this cascading process, one target can initiate repeated hairpin hybridisation, thereby amplifying the signal produced by the detection system.

Rationale for Selecting HCR

Directly visible colorimetric readout. HCR products can include G-rich sequences that form G-quadruplex structures. These structures bind hemin and catalyse the oxidation of TMBS to generate a visible colour change. Compared with the eGFP-based toehold assay, which requires excitation at a specific wavelength and fluorescence-detection equipment, this colorimetric approach allows the result to be observed directly under normal lighting conditions, aligning with our aim of developing a home test kit.

Signal amplification for low-abundance miRNA. A small amount of target miRNA can initiate cascading hybridisation of multiple hairpin probes, producing a much stronger signal from the initial target. This allows low-abundance miRNA to be converted into a more visible readout, which is particularly important for early-stage detection.

Lower detection limits. Enhanced HCR methods, such as toehold-mediated exponential HCR, can achieve detection limits as low as 92.7 fM, enabling sensitive detection of low-abundance miRNA biomarkers associated with early diabetic nephropathy.

High specificity and low background. HCR uses multiple, specific hairpins, making it highly effective at distinguishing single-base mismatches among miRNA sequences. This selectivity is crucial given the high homology among miRNA families.

Limitations: slower reaction kinetics than enzyme-based amplification; background leakage (hairpins may spontaneously open and produce false positives); limited multiplexing (cross-talk between amplification pathways).

Build 1: Constructing H1 and H2 hairpins + NUPACK analysis

When miRNA target (miR-21 / miR-192) is present, the H1 hairpin binds to the complementary miRNA target and becomes linear, exposing its sequence complementary to H2. The H2 hairpin binds to the complementary H1 sequence and becomes linear. H1 and H2 binding goes on and on to form long H1–H2 complexes. A special secondary structure called a G-quadruplex (G4) is formed in the H1–H2 complexes.

Test 1: Testing sensitivity of H1 and H2 hairpins with PAGE

To test the sensitivity of the HCR hairpins, we first conducted gel electrophoresis using agarose gel, with miRNA concentrations ranging from 0 nM, 1 nM, 10 nM, 100 nM to 1 μM. With the large difference in miRNA concentrations in this experiment, we would be able to determine the range that has significant formation of hybrids. However, after conducting gel electrophoresis, it was observed that all lanes had smearing. This indicates that even in the setup with 0 nM miRNA concentration, the H1 and H2 hairpins may have bonded with each other, giving an unobservable result.

Possible explanations include:

  • The hairpin structures are too stable to be opened by the miRNA trigger.
  • As agarose gel has large pores, complexes may not be filtered out by the agarose gel, producing unobservable results.

To address these issues, polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) was employed in subsequent experiments. PAGE offers considerably smaller pore sizes than agarose gels and should more effectively separate small nucleotide species and individual DNA strands, yielding more accurate and interpretable results.

Learn 1

  • PAGE and agarose gel failed to produce an observable sensitivity result.
  • Considered other methods to detect hairpin sensitivity.

Build 2: Building hairpins that contain InG4 sequences

For the construction of H1 and H2 hairpins for the miRNAs, we took reference from the literature on a new way of HCR — a double-loop hairpin, where InG4 sequences are located in the loops of the hairpins.

Design criteria for HCR-G4 hairpins:

  1. A high concentration of hybrids is formed, ensuring the HCR reaction has occurred successfully and the InG4 sequences are inside a stable complex such that they can dissociate from the hybrids to form G-quadruplexes.
  2. Most InG4 sequences are located in the loops inside the hybrid. Once InG4 sequences are in the stems and base-paired, the GGG bases would not form the G-quadruplex. Only when the InG4 sequences are located inside the loops can they leave the long polymer and form G-quadruplexes.
  3. Little H1–H2 complex is found without miRNA. If H1 and H2 bind each other in the closed state / without the presence of miRNA, false positives may be produced.

First, to construct the H1 hairpin, a complementary stem of the miR-192-5p was built to allow the miRNA to bind with the hairpin. Then, to build the H1–H2 binding site and the zipped-up stem for the hairpin, a part of the miRNA was added to bind with the complementary stem, leaving an exposed part for the miRNA to bind with. In between, a loop was built to insert the InG4 sequences into the hairpins for further action.

Then to construct the H2 hairpin, a complementary sequence to the H1–H2 binding site of H1 was built so H2 can bind with the H1 hairpin in the open state. InG4 sequences are also included inside the loops to allow the G bases to leave the large complex and form G-quadruplexes. Lastly, to build a H2–H1 binding site, a sequence identical to miR-192-5p was added to the hairpin to allow H2 to bind with H1, cascading thousands of times and producing a very long polymer.

Validating our samples with NUPACK. We used NUPACK to test many HCR hairpin designs, searching for the best HCR design.

Test 2: Testing HCR hairpins with ThT fluorescence assay

The G-quadruplexes formed by H1–H2 complexes alone are invisible to the naked eye and give no detectable signal upon formation. Chemicals must be added to confirm the formation and generate detectable signals. G4-quadruplex structures formed by DNA or RNA can specifically bind the light-up fluorescent dye Thioflavin T (ThT). Free ThT in solution exhibits very low fluorescence. However, upon binding to G4 complexes, ThT undergoes a significant increase in fluorescence emission compared with binding to other DNA/RNA forms (Xu et al., 2016). As a result, ThT serves as a useful probe for distinguishing G4-quadruplexes from non-G4 structures.

Figure 23: Graph of HCR ThT signal with miRNA-21 and miRNA-192

In the ThT fluorescence HCR experiments, miR-21 reproduced the expected pattern from the literature: ThT alone is weakly fluorescent, but binding to G4 structures gives a marked signal increase. Specifically, 100 pM, 10 nM and 100 nM miR-21 all produced significantly higher fluorescence than the 0 pM control. On replicate G4/ThT runs analyzed by repeated-measures one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s post-hoc (n=4), the trend persisted, with 10 nM and 100 nM again significantly different from control, supporting that the H1/H2 polymerization and G4 quadruplex formation are working as intended for miR-21. Meanwhile, miR-192 could not show the expected increasing trend; fluorescence intensity plateaued at roughly the same level across increasing miR-192 concentrations, with no significant correlation.

Learn 2: Enhancing signal detection with Hemin-TMBS assay

After evaluating the ThT-coupled HCR system, we identified the following limitations that may reduce its suitability for a practical at-home diagnostic kit. To address this, we transitioned to the hemin/TMBS-coupled HCR assay as an alternative detection strategy.

No requirement for a fluorescence reader. Fluorescence enhancement of ThT upon binding to G-quadruplexes requires UV light excitation and a fluorescence reader to visualise the signal (Renaud de la Faverie et al., 2014), which reduces practicality for an at-home test kit. In the hemin/TMBS system, the G-quadruplex/hemin DNAzyme catalyses the oxidation of TMBS, producing a clear visible colour change that can be observed directly by the naked eye (Li et al., 2009).

Stronger signal amplification. ThT produces fluorescence upon binding G4-quadruplexes, but each G4-quadruplex can only light up a limited number of ThT molecules (Renaud de la Faverie et al., 2014). In contrast, the G4-quadruplex binds hemin to form a peroxidase-mimicking DNAzyme (Nasab et al., 2016). This enzyme continuously oxidises TMBS in the presence of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). Since the reaction is catalytic, a single G4/hemin complex can repeatedly oxidise many TMBS molecules (Li et al., 2021), producing a much higher effective signal output.

Stabilisation of G4-quadruplexes. Hemin stabilises G4-quadruplex structures much better than ThT because of how the two molecules fit onto the DNA (Renaud de la Faverie et al., 2014). ThT is much smaller and only connects to a small part of the structure with weaker force (Nasab et al., 2016). In contrast, hemin has a large, flat shape that matches the top of the G-quadruplex (Li et al., 2021). When hemin sticks to the DNA, it covers a wide area and binds very tightly, locking the structure in place (Tao et al., 2024).

Test 3: Testing HCR hairpins with Hemin and TMBS assay

First, hemin (an iron-containing compound) is added to the H1–H2 complexes. When hemin binds to G-quadruplexes, this enables the G-quadruplexes to gain a similar function to enzymes, catalysing reactions involving colour change. TMBS is a colourless substrate. When TMBS is added to the G-quadruplex/hemin (G4-hemin) complex, the TMBS is oxidised and turns the mixture from colourless to a distinct blue colour. Finally, sulphuric acid (H2SO4) is added to stop the reaction; the mixture turns from blue to yellow, allowing us to measure absorbance at 450 nm.

Figure 24: HCR reactions with miRNA-21 and miRNA-192 at varying concentrations

Figure 25: Graph of color intensity of HCR reactions with miRNA-21 and miRNA-192

In the HCR assay with miR-21, the five wells containing H1, H2 and increasing concentrations of miR-21 all developed a stronger yellow colour compared with the control wells (blank, H1 alone, H2 alone, miRNA alone and H1+H2). From 1 nM to 100 nM, a clear concentration-dependent increase in colour intensity was observed. This demonstrates that higher concentrations of miR-21 triggered more extensive HCR, leading to greater formation of G-quadruplexes available for the hemin/TMBS reaction and thus stronger colour output. For the HCR assay with miR-192, the same concentration series (10 pM to 100 nM) produced a different pattern. The yellow colour intensity increased from 10 pM to 100 pM, but then decreased up to 100 nM.

Learn 3: Self-amplification of HCR hairpin H1

Notably, for miR-21, H1 alone, H2 alone and the initiator alone did not produce a visible colour change. Nevertheless, for miR-192, H1 alone caused a comparable colour change. This observation suggests that the miR-192 G4H1 alone might have undergone undesirable spontaneous self-amplification, leading to the formation of G-quadruplex structures even in the absence of the miRNA target.

Possible reasons for miR-192 G4H1 self-amplification:

  • Intrinsic tendency of the G-rich sequence to form G-quadruplexes. Even when the H1 hairpin is closed, partial unfolding can expose enough of the G-rich sequence for intramolecular or intermolecular G4 formation (Burge et al., 2006; Neidle & Parkinson, 2003). Once a small number of G4 structures form, they can bind hemin and generate colour, creating a visible background signal.
  • Insufficient thermodynamic stability of the hairpin stem. The stem of the designed H1 hairpin may be too short or contain too few G–C base pairs, so the free-energy barrier for spontaneous opening is low. Under the ionic conditions used for HCR and G4 stabilisation, the hairpin might open transiently, allowing the G4 domain to fold without any target trigger (Wang et al., 2020; Ang & Yung, 2016).
  • H1–H1 intermolecular interactions. At the working concentration of H1, molecules may interact with one another through complementary regions or G-rich stretches, leading to partial activation of the cascade in the absence of miR-192 (Wang et al., 2020; Choi et al., 2014).

Cycle 5: Modification on HCR G4 hairpins

Design and Build: Modifying G4 hairpins to prevent self-amplification

To suppress self-amplification and background leakage in our HCR system, we can engineer G4 hairpins with enhanced stability in the closed state while preserving efficient miRNA-triggered opening in the open state. Strategies for modifying the HCR H1 hairpin for miR-192:

  • Extend stem lengths by 2–4 base pairs to increase the thermodynamic barrier against spontaneous hairpin opening.
  • Introduce G·C-rich clamps at stem termini to further stabilise these vulnerable regions without blocking toehold access.
  • Reposition InG4 sequences deeper within loop regions to prevent inter-hairpin G-quadruplex formation that could nucleate unintended polymerisation in the absence of target miRNA.
  • Optimise toehold lengths to 18–22 nucleotides, increasing the overall free-energy barrier to ensure rapid, specific initiation only upon binding cognate miR-21-5p or miR-192-5p, minimising non-specific interactions.
