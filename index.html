Cycle 1: Using toehold switches to detect CKD

Design: Justifying the use of toehold switches + Choosing miRNAs

Inspiration for the Project

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is a prevalent disease in Hong Kong affecting around 10% of our population. The scale of this problem is ever growing. CKD cases in Hong Kong soared by over 180,000 from 2021 to 2025. This sharp increase leads to three major issues: a heavy strain on dialysis units, significant financial burdens for patients, and a high death rate. In 2024, kidney-related conditions were the 6th leading cause of death in Hong Kong, with 1,700 deaths, and 70% of it is related to CKD. Early detection is crucial for saving lives and alleviating pressure on the healthcare system.

What is miRNA?

miRNA (microRNA) is a type of ribonucleic acid found in plants and animals. It acts like a control switch which binds to messenger RNA to turn genes off, blocking protein production. miRNA levels may be upregulated or downregulated when a patient is infected with diseases, and may also contribute to conditions such as cancer and heart problems.

Why did we choose miRNA as our detection target?

miRNAs are often upregulated or downregulated in diseased states and act as a reporter for specific diseases, including but not limited to:

  • Cancers
  • Cardiovascular disease
  • Neurological diseases
  • Infectious diseases

Pros and Cons of miRNA as a Disease Reporter

Pros Cons
High stability Enclosed in microvesicles or bound to proteins, miRNAs resist degradation by enzymes (RNases) in blood and urine. Complex signals One miRNA targets many genes, and one gene is controlled by many miRNAs, clouding disease-specific signs.
Early warning Their levels often shift before standard clinical indicators, such as serum creatinine or significant albuminuria, show measurable changes. Outside interference Inflammation, other illnesses, and common drugs alter miRNA levels, causing false readings.
Disease specificity Specific patterns of dysregulated miRNAs correspond directly to underlying pathological processes like renal fibrosis, inflammation, and diabetic nephropathy.
Non-invasive access Urinary and circulating miRNAs can be sampled easily and repeatedly without requiring painful or risky kidney biopsies.

Why did we consider a multi-miRNA system?

We acknowledged that using only one miRNA will not be sufficient to detect CKD. A multi-miRNA system uses several miRNAs together, improving disease detection, boosting gene knockdown, and targeting complex biological pathways, which is much better than using a single-miRNA panel, as miRNAs are not specific enough to detect only CKD, the accuracy of having a single-miRNA panel to detect CKD will be way lower than having a multi-miRNA system.

Since CKD is not a single disease, but the final common pathway of several underlying conditions, no miRNA is totally specific to CKD. Therefore, we adopted a multi-miRNA system to check for its most common pathways for CKD, namely:

  • Hypertensive nephropathy
  • Diabetic nephropathy

In this situation, using a multi-miRNA system increases reliability and accuracy for the detection of CKD.

Which miRNAs did we choose?

In our original screening we’ve found 5 miRNAs related to CKD, with it being miR-21-5p, miR-192-5p, miR-204-5p, miR-214-3p and miR-193a-3p. However, our target miRNA for this project needed to meet the following 2 criteria:

  • Shows significant upregulation of concentration compared to healthy controls in the urine samples of CKD patients
  • Needs to be caught in early to mid stages of CKD

During the second stage of our literature review, we discovered that miR-204-5p and miR-214-3p appeared to be downregulated instead of being upregulated, making it difficult to detect via our current toehold design. Among the remaining 3 miRNAs, miR-193-3p is found to be only upregulated by 1.4-fold. Compared to miR-21-5p’s 2-5 times increase and miR-192-5p’s 3 times increase, the choice is clear and our final choice landed on miR-21-5p and miR-192-5p.

Limitations of current medical detection methods (eGFR and uACR)

  • Delayed detection: eGFR only measures the filtration rate rather than structural kidney damage. Significant kidney injury can occur long before the eGFR drops below normal levels.
  • Misses early damage: Standard cutoffs classify uACR levels below 30 mg/g as completely normal. However, early kidney damage and cardiovascular risk can progress below this threshold.

What is a Toehold Switch?

A toehold switch is a type of detection method for the upregulation of miRNA. Our team has used a toehold switch mechanism to create a test kit for CKD. Below are the mechanisms, advantages and disadvantages of using toehold switches.

Mechanism of Toehold Switches

Toehold switches are de-novo-designed synthetic riboregulators (custom-made RNA switches from sketch to turn bacterial genes on/off) that activate gene expression in response to cognate RNAs with designed sequences. The toehold switch miRNA has a ribosome-binding site (RBS) and a start codon hidden inside the hairpin. When there is no trigger miRNA present, the ribosome cannot bind to the RBS, and hence no reporter protein will be produced. However, when the specific trigger miRNA binds to the toehold at the 5’end, it opens the hairpin by branch migration; therefore, ribosomes can bind to the RBS, starting translation and producing reporter proteins.

Rationale for Selecting Toehold Switches

  • High specificity: detects exact 22-nucleotide miRNA sequences
  • Non-invasive sampling: works with urine = blood draw not needed
  • Programmability: Toehold switches are highly programmable, enabling precise gene regulation and simultaneous detection of multiple miRNA targets
  • minimal crosstalk: Toehold switches can be designed with different sequences, so each trigger usually interacts only with its matching switch
  • Low-cost format: Can be deployed on paper test strips

Limitations
Weak Signals at Early Disease Stages: Toehold switches may have limited sensitivity for detecting low-abundance miRNAs in early-stage disease

Build: Constructing toehold switches by using miRADAR

Introduction to miRADAR (from 2024 Wageningen iGEM project)

miRADAR explores the use of miRNAs as potential biomarkers for detection. As miRNA expression profiles may differ between healthy individuals and people with MS, these small RNA molecules could provide useful molecular signals for future diagnostic applications. The system uses RNA-responsive toehold switches to detect miRNAs and convert their presence into a measurable output. Therefore, we computationally designed toehold switches targeting an associated miRNA and experimentally evaluated their performance in the laboratory. These experiments assessed whether computationally designed, miRNA-responsive toehold switches could function as RNA sensors.

Test: Using NUPACK to analyse our toehold switches.

For the dry lab component, we have made use of the miRADAR (from the 2024 Wageningen iGEM team) and the app NUPACK, to generate and evaluate sample toehold switches for our miR-21-5p and miR-192-5p.

We have put all of the samples into NUPACK at 37 degree celsius for validating, with the RNA mode while toehold concentration of 1 μM and miRNA concentration of 3 μM.

Criteria for choosing suitable toehold switches:

  1. A high concentration of bound toeholds is formed. This ensures that the miRNA is able to bind to the toehold switch and unwind the hairpin stem to release the ribosome binding site for ribosome to bind with and produce eGFP (enhanced green fluorescence protein)
  2. No/Little unintended secondary structures are formed in the bound toeholds. When there are unintended secondary structures and base-pairing formed in the bound toeholds after the ribosome binding site, they would block the ribosome from reaching the protein coding sequence and produce the eGFP protein. Hence, false negative results may occur.
  3. HMFE vs TMFE. HMFE (hybrid minimum free energy) and TMFE (toehold minimum free energy) are one of the main factors that we would have to assess. If the TMFE is lower than the HMFE, the toeholds would prioritize binding with another toehold switch, instead of producing hybrids to detect eGFP.

We based the choice on our toehold designs of 5 criteria:

  • Efficient and high concentration toehold switch binding to miRNA targets,
  • Stable closed toehold structure in unbound state, minimising spontaneous self-linearisation in the absence of miRNA target and the risk of false-positive signals,
  • Lower equilibrium probability of the bonds holding the toehold switch loop structures when bonded with miRNA targets, allowing the loop structures in the toehold switch to be readily linearised to trigger reporter protein synthesis,
  • Lower Minimum Free Energy of bound toehold with miRNA (HMFE) than unbounded toeholds (TMFE), allowing unbound toehold to be more readily transit to bound toehold state,
  • The Ribosome Binding Site (RBS) needs to be located within the loop structure ot toehold to reduce risk of ribosome binding to toehold in absence of miRNA targets.

Bearing these principles in mind and analysis with NUPACK simulation, two toehold candidates emerged as the strongest performers for each miRNA target: Toeholds 6 and 7 for miR-192, and Toeholds 7 and 9 for miR-21.

As shown in the NUPACK analysis in Figure 16 (the red bar graphs), compared with the other designs, Toeholds 7 and 9 for miR-21 produce a higher concentration of the active toehold–miRNA complex. This strong binding ensures that very little miRNA remains unbound, maximising signal output and reducing the likelihood of false negatives.

Toehold 7 and 9 for miR-21 are having high equilibrium probability in unbound state (Figure 14), showing that they contain relatively stable internal bonds and will not be so readily linearised without a trigger miRNA target. In addition, the base-pairing interactions within the loop region of Toeholds 7 and 9 in bounded state (Figure 15) were designed to have a low equilibrium probability (indicated by the light-blue dots in the NUPACK diagrams). Once the miRNA target binds, the base pairs that maintain the secondary loop structures shift to a lower equilibrium probability. This destabilises the loops, allowing the toehold switch to linearise fully and expose the RBS so that the ribosome can initiate reporter protein synthesis.

As shown in the above Figure 16, the selected toehold candidates also possess a lower minimum free energy while bonded (HMFE) as opposed to being unbounded (TMFE), this complies with thermodynamics, energetically favoring the bound state.

Finally, in the unbound state of both toehold 7 and 9 for miR-21, the ribosome binding site (RBS) is sequestered within a loop structure rather than an exposed linear conformation. This minimizes unintended ribosome binding and background expression in the absence of the target miRNA.

Cycle 2: Experimenting toehold switches

Design: Cloning rationale for eGFP

Plasmid selection

We selected plasmid pET-15b-EGFP as our plasmids. On one hand, it contains a T7 promoter, which utilizes T7 RNA polymerase which drives strong and inducible reactions and is superior to typical host bacterial enzymes. On the other hand, an eGFP reporter gene is included to produce a sensitive and quantifiable output, while a T7 terminator is included to stop the RNA production. Besides, the plasmid also contains a AmpR gene which provides the bacteria with ampicillin resistance such that it may still survive in the agar plates containing ampicillin.

Figure 17: Overview of plasmid pET-15b-EGFP

Cloning of plasmid (Digestion)

In the plasmid pET-15b-EGFP, we utilized two restriction enzyme sites XbaI and NdeI. The restriction enzyme XbaI recognizes and cleaves the sequence 5’TCTAGA3’, while restriction enzyme NdeI recognizes and cleaves the sequence 5’CATATG 3’. As both of these restriction sites are downstream to the T7 promoter and upstream to the eGFP reporter, it allows the T7 promoter to synthesize our toehold switch, the eGFP reporter to be expressed and the T7 terminator to stop synthesizing the toehold switch.

Test: eGFP cell free expression assay

Mechanism of EGFP cell free expression

The EGFP cell free expression makes use of a fluorometer to detect the specific fluorescence inside the samples. To initiate the reaction, the fluorometer expresses a specific wavelength onto the sample such that the electrons inside the sample absorb the energy, which will then emit waves with longer wavelengths and little energy. These waves could then be detected by the fluorometer through a GFP filter.

Cycle 3: Using mCherry to produce colorimetric result

Design: Cloning rationale of mCherry plasmid

To improve the user experience and practical accessibility of our diagnostic kit, we hypothesized that altering our reporter protein would yield a more intuitive and user-friendly readout. The primary objective of this cycle was to redesign our plasmid architecture to incorporate mCherry as the designated reporter. The rationale driving this modification is that mCherry, upon adequate expression and cellular accumulation, generates a distinct colorimetric output that is visible under standard ambient lighting. Learn: From toehold switch to HCR

Lack of visible result from toehold switch

Through evaluation of our original toehold-switch design, we identified a practical limitation associated with using eGFP as the reporter. Although successful activation of the toehold switch can lead to eGFP production, the resulting fluorescence is not readily visible under normal lighting conditions and generally requires appropriate excitation with a specific wavelength and fluorescence-detection equipment to observe.

This means that successful molecular detection does not necessarily translate into a result that can be easily interpreted by the user.

From this, we learned that the current method is effective at target recognition, but lacks a directly visible method for signal visualization.

Insufficient sensitivity at low miRNA concentration

mCherry was considered to be an alternative to eGFP since sufficiently accumulated mCherry can produce a visible colour under normal lighting conditions.

However, achieving a clearly visible colour requires substantial reporter accumulation. This exposes another hurdle.

At low target miRNA concentrations, reporter production may be insufficient to generate a perceivable output. This is particularly relevant to our aim of detecting disease at the early-to-mid stage, where the abundance of the selected miRNA biomarkers may be relatively low. Therefore, we identified signal amplification as an additional design requirement to improve detection sensitivity.

Two identified design challenges

Through this analysis, we recognised that an effective diagnostic system needs to satisfy two requirements:

  1. a readout that can be easily observed
  2. sufficient signal amplification at low target miRNA concentration

Cycle 4: From toehold switches to using HCR to detect miRNA

Design: Justifying the use of HCR

After evaluating the limitations of reporter-based toehold switches, we explored Hybridization Chain Reaction (HCR) as an alternative miRNA detection method.

Mechanism of HCR

HCR is an enzyme-free signal amplification technique that relies on target-triggered hybridisation between designed DNA hairpins, resulting in the formation of long nicked double-stranded DNA polymers.

By using two complementary hairpins, the opening of one hairpin exposes a sequence that can trigger the opening of the next, allowing the reaction to continue in a chain. Through this cascading process, one target can initiate repeated hairpin hybridisation, thereby amplifying the signal produced by the detection system.

Rationale for Selecting HCR as an alternative:

Directly Visible Colorimetric Readout

One advantage of HCR is that the amplified products can be combined with alternative signal-output mechanisms rather than relying on fluorescent reporter proteins.

In our design, HCR produces long H1–H2 DNA complexes containing G-rich sequences that form G-quadruplex structures. These G-quadruplex structures can bind to hemin and catalyse the oxidation of TMBS to generate a visible colour change.

Compared with the eGFP-based toehold assay, which requires excitation at a specific wavelength and fluorescence-detection equipment to observe the output, this colorimetric approach allows the result to be observed directly under normal lighting conditions, aligning with our aim of developing a home test kit.

Signal amplification for low-abundance miRNA

Another advantage of HCR is its ability to amplify the detection signal through a cascading hybridisation reaction, allowing a small amount of target miRNA to initiate repeated opening and binding of multiple hairpin probes.

A single target miRNA initiates the formation of long H1–H2 DNA complexes through successive hairpin hybridisation. This amplification process increases the amount of signal-generating product formed from a relatively low concentration of target miRNA.

At the early stage of disease, the concentration of the target miRNA may still be relatively low. With a mCherry-based toehold system, low target concentration may activate only a limited number of switches, resulting in insufficient reporter production to generate a clearly visible colour.

HCR addresses this limitation through signal amplification. A small amount of target miRNA can initiate a cascading hybridisation reaction involving multiple hairpin probes, producing a much stronger signal from the initial target. This allows low-abundance miRNA to be converted into a more visible readout, which is particularly important for early-stage detection.

Lower Detection Limits

Enhanced HCR methods, such as toehold-mediated exponential HCR, can achieve detection limits as low as 92.7 fM, enabling sensitive detection of low-abundance miRNA biomarkers associated with early diabetic nephropathy.

High Specificity and Low Background: HCR uses multiple, specific hairpins, making it highly effective at distinguishing single-base mismatches among miRNA sequences. This selectivity is crucial given the high homology among miRNA families.

Limitations

  1. Slow reaction kinetics: HCR generally proceeds more slowly than enzyme-based amplification methods
  2. Background leakage: Hairpins may spontaneously open without the target initiator, producing false-positive signals and reducing sensitivity.
  3. Limited multiplexing: Detecting multiple targets can lead to cross-talk between amplification pathways.

Build 1: Constructing H1 and H2 hairpins + NUPACK analysis/ Rationale of sensitivity testing of H1 and H2 hairpins.

Formation of H1-H2 complexes

When miRNA target (miR21/ miR192) is present, H1 hairpin binds to complementary miRNA target and becomes linear, exposing its sequence complementary to H2. H2 hairpin binds to complementary H1 sequences and becomes linear. H1 and H2 binding goes on and on to form long H1-H2 complexes. A special secondary structure called G-quadruplex (G4) is formed in the H1-H2 complexes.

Test 1: Testing sensitivity of H1 and H2 hairpins with PAGE

Sensitivity of HCR hairpins

To test for the sensitivity of HCR hairpins, we first conducted gel electrophoresis, using agarose gel. With miRNA concentrations ranging from 0 nM, 1 nM, 10 nM, 100 nM, 1 μM. With the large difference in miRNA concentrations in this experiment, we would be able to determine the range that has a significant formation of hybrids. However, after conducting gel electrophoresis, it is observed that all runways have smearings. This indicates that even in the setup with 0 nM of miRNA concentration, the H1 and H2 bonds may have bonded with each other and an unobservable result has been discovered.

Possible explanations include:

  • The hairpin structures are too stable to be opened by the miRNA trigger
  • As agarose gel has large pores, complexes may not be able to be filtered out by the agarose gel. Therefore, an unobservable result is produced in lanes 5-8.

To address these issues, polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) was employed in subsequent experiments, where instead of agarose gel, PAGE offers considerably smaller pore sizes than agarose gels. This higher-resolution matrix should more effectively separate small nucleotide species and individual DNA strands, yielding more accurate and interpretable results.

Build 2: Building hairpins that contain InG4 sequences to detect upregulation changes.

For the construction of H1 and H2 hairpins for the miRNAs, we would take reference to the literature published by (XXX).

This literature highlights a new way of HCR–A double loop hairpin, where InG4 sequences are located in the loops of the hairpins.

Design Criteria for HCR-G4 hairpins

  1. A high concentration of hybrids formed. This ensures that the HCR reaction has occurred successfully, where the InG4 sequences are now inside a stable complex such that they are able to dissociate from the hybrids to form G-quadruplexes.
  2. Most InG4 sequences are located in the loops inside the hybrid. Once the InG4 sequences are located inside the stems and are binded with another base. The GGG bases would not form the G-quadruplex as they are the ones to support the polymer formation. Only when the InG4 sequences are located inside the loops would they be able to leave the long polymer and form G-quadruplexes.
  3. Little H1-H2 complexes are found. If H1 hairpin and H2 hairpin bind with each other in closed state/without the presence of miRNA, false positives may be produced as complexes are formed.

Criteria that we would take reference to when picking candidates:

As we could take reference to the literature for the miR-21-5p hairpin, we have also constructed a miR-192-5p H1 and H2 hairpin.

First, to construct the H1 hairpin, a complementary stem of the miR-192-5p was built to allow the miRNA to bind with the hairpin. Then, to build the H1-H2 binding site and to build up the zipped up stem for the hairpin, a part of the miRNA was added to bind with the complementary stem, leaving an exposed part for the miRNA to bind with. In between, a loop was built to insert the InG4 sequences into the hairpins for further action.

Then to construct the H2 hairpin, a complementary sequence to the H1-H2 binding site of H1 was built to bind H2 with the H1 hairpin in open state. Then, InG4 sequences are also included inside the loops to allow the G bases to leave the large complex and form G-quadruplexes. Lastly, to build a H2-H1 binding site, a sequence that is identical to the miR-192-5p was added to the hairpin to allow H2 to bind with H1, cascading thousands of times and producing a very long polymer.

Test 2: Testing HCR hairpins with ThT fluorescence assay

The G4-quadruplexes formed by H1-H2 complexes alone are invisible to the naked eye and give no detectable signal upon formation. Chemicals have to be further added to confirm the formation and generate detectable signals.

Mechanism of signal output when adding ThT to HCR G4-quadruplexes

G4-quadruplex structures formed by DNA or RNA can specifically bind the light-up fluorescent dye Thioflavin T (ThT). Free ThT in solution exhibits very low fluorescence. However, upon binding to G4 complexes, ThT undergoes a significant increase in fluorescence emission compared with binding to other DNA/RNA forms (Xu et al., 2016). As a result, ThT serves as a useful probe for distinguishing G4-quadruplexes from non-G4 structures. Consequently, upon detection of an increased fluorescence signal from ThT, this enables us to confirm the presence of G4 structures generated by HCR, thereby verifying the successful detection of the miRNA target that triggers G4 formation.

Figure 24: Graph of color intensity of HCR reactions with miRNA-21 and miRNA-192

In the ThT fluorescence HCR experiments, miR-21 reproduced the expected pattern from the literature: ThT alone is weakly fluorescent, but binding to G4 structures gives a marked signal increase, specifically 100 pM, 10 nM and 100 nM miR-21 all produced significantly higher fluorescence than the 0 pM control. On replicate G4/ThT runs analyzed by repeated-measures one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s post-hoc (n=4), the trend persisted, with 10 nM and 100 nM again significantly different from control, supporting that the H1/H2 polymerization and G4 quadruplex formation are working as intended for miR-21. Meanwhile, miR-192 could not show the expected increasing trend, fluorescence intensity plateaued at roughly the same level across increasing miR-192 concentrations, with no significant correlation.

Learn 2: Enhancing Signal Amplification and Convenience of Signal Detection by using Hemin-TMBS assay as alternative

After evaluating the ThT-coupled HCR system, we identified the following limitations that may reduce its suitability for a practical at-home diagnostic kit. To address this, we transitioned to the hemin/TMBS-coupled HCR assay as an alternative detection strategy.

No requirement for fluorescence reader

Fluorescence enhancement of ThT upon binding to G-quadruplexes requires UV light excitation and a fluorescence reader to visualise the signal (Renaud de la Faverie et al., 2014), which reduces practicality for an at-home test kit. To overcome this limitation, we selected the hemin and TMBS-coupled HCR system instead. In this method, the G-quadruplex/hemin DNAzyme catalyses the oxidation of TMBS, producing a clear visible colour change that can be observed directly by the naked eye (Li et al., 2009). This eliminates the need for any specialised equipment such as a fluorescence reader or UV light source, making the test kit significantly more user-friendly, accessible, and suitable for household use.

Stronger signal amplification

ThT produces fluorescence upon binding G4-quadruplexes, but each G4-quadruplex can only light up a limited number of ThT molecules by locking its central bond in place (Renaud de la Faverie et al., 2014).

In contrast, the G4-quadruplex binds hemin to form a peroxidase-mimicking DNAzyme (Nasab et al., 2016). This enzyme continuously oxidises TMBS in the presence of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). Since the reaction is catalytic, a single G4/hemin complex can repeatedly oxidise many TMBS molecules, generating a large amount of coloured product from only a small number of G4 structures (Li et al., 2021). This catalytic turnover produces a much higher effective signal output than the simple stoichiometric binding of ThT. This fundamental difference in signal generation is the key reason hemin/TMBS systems deliver stronger and more easily detected signals than ThT-based reporters. When the catalytic amplification of hemin/TMBS is combined with Hybridization Chain Reaction (HCR), which already multiplies the number of G4 units, the overall sensitivity and practical visibility of the signal are significantly enhanced (Colorimetric and photothermal dual-modal analysis of a cancer-related gene based on G-quadruplex-hemin cascade signal amplification - new journal of chemistry (RSC Publishing)).

Stabilisation on G4-quadruplexes

Hemin stabilizes G4-quadruplex structures much better than ThT because of how the two molecules fit onto the DNA (Renaud de la Faverie et al., 2014). ThT is a much smaller molecule that only connects to a small part of the structure with weaker force (Nasab et al., 2016). In contrast, hemin has a large, flat shape that matches the top of the G-quadruplex (Li et al., 2021). When hemin sticks to the DNA, it covers a wide area and binds very tightly, which locks the structure in place and makes it less easy to break apart. Because of this, hemin is far better at pulling DNA into a stable G-quadruplex shape and keeping it secure (Tao et al., 2024).

Test 3: Testing HCR hairpins with Hemin and TMBS assay

First, hemin (an iron-containing compound) is added to the H1-H2 complexes. When hemin binds to G4-quadruplexes, this enables the G4-quadruplexes to gain a similar function as enzymes, which catalyse reactions involving colour change.

TMBS is a colourless substrate. When TMBS is added to the G-quadruplex/hemin (G4-hemin) complex, the TMBS is being oxidised and turns the mixture from colorless to a distinct blue colour. Finally, sulphuric acid (H2SO4) is added to stop the reaction, the mixture turns from blue to yellow which we can measure the absorbance reading at 450 nm.

Figure 25: Results of HCR reactions with miRNA-21 and miRNA-192 at varying concentrations

Figure 26: Graph of color intensity of HCR reactions with miRNA-21 and miRNA-192 at increasing concentrations.

In the HCR assay with miR-21, the five wells containing H1, H2 and increasing concentrations of miR-21 all developed a stronger yellow colour compared with the control wells (blank, H1 alone, H2 alone, miRNA alone and H1+H2). From 1 nM to 100 nM, a clear concentration-dependent increase in colour intensity was observed, as shown in Figure 25 and Figure 26 (graph). This demonstrates that higher concentrations of miR-21 actually triggered more extensive HCR, leading to greater formation of G-quadruplexes available for the hemin/TMB reaction and thus stronger colour output.

For the HCR assay with miR-192, the same concentration series (10 pM to 100 nM) produced a different pattern. The yellow colour intensity increased from 10 pM to 100 pM, but then decreased up to 100 nM.

Learn 3: Acknowledged self-amplification of HCR hairpin H1.

Notably, as shown in Figure 25, for miR-21, H1 alone, H2 alone and the initiator alone did not produce a visible colour change. Nevertheless, for miR-192, H1 alone caused a comparable colour change. This observation suggests that the miR-192 G4H1 alone might have undergone undesirable spontaneous self-amplification, leading to the formation of G-quadruplex structures even in the absence of the miRNA target.

Possible Reasons for miR-192 G4H1 Self-Amplification

Intrinsic tendency of the G-rich sequence to form G-quadruplexes

The G4-quadruplex-forming sequence in H1 is guanine-rich. Even when the H1 hairpin is closed, partial unfolding can expose enough of the G-rich sequence for intramolecular or intermolecular G4 formation (Burge et al., 2006; Neidle & Parkinson, 2003). Once a small number of G4 structures form, they can bind hemin and generate colour, creating a visible background signal.

Insufficient thermodynamic stability of the hairpin stem

The stem of the designed H1 hairpin may be too short or contain too few G–C base pairs, so the free-energy barrier for spontaneous opening is low. Under the ionic conditions used for HCR and G4 stabilisation, the hairpin might open transiently, allowing the G4 domain to fold without any target trigger (Wang et al., 2020; Ang & Yung, 2016).

H1–H1 intermolecular interactions

At the working concentration of H1, molecules may interact with one another through complementary regions or G-rich stretches, leading to partial activation of the cascade in the absence of miR-192 (Wang et al., 2020; Choi et al., 2014).

Cycle 5: Modification on HCR G4 hairpins

Design and Build: Modifying G4 hairpins to prevent self-amplification.

Strategies for modifying the HCR H1 hairpin for miR-192

To suppress self-amplification and background leakage in our HCR system, we can engineer G4 hairpins with enhanced stability in the closed state while preserving efficient miRNA-triggered opening in the open state. We can extend stem lengths by 2–4 base pairs to increase the thermodynamic barrier against spontaneous hairpin opening, and introduce G·C-rich clamps at stem termini to further stabilize these vulnerable regions without blocking toehold access. InG4 sequences can be repositioned deeper within loop regions to prevent inter-hairpin G-quadruplex formation that could nucleate unintended polymerization in the absence of target miRNA. Additionally, toehold lengths should be optimized to 18–22 nucleotides increasing the overall free energy barrier to ensure rapid, specific initiation only upon binding cognate miR-21-5p or miR-192-5p, minimizing non-specific interactions.
